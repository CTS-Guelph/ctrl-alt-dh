A platform can be many things; a physical object to stand upon, a means in which to share your speech, a form of opportunity and many more meanings in between. All share the same underlying meaning - a way to bolster yourself upwards. This idea is what Tarleton Gillespie speaks about when regarding multi-million-dollar companies strategically centering themselves and governing over our media, all the while protecting and hurting those who use their system. This article highlights the differences in class among consumers as companies like Google and YouTube purposely situate themselves to ensure current and future economic gain at the expense of the common person. These companies lie between the protection of specific legislations while simultaneously warping them to their own benefit. Large corporations like these rely on the people yet they are the ones who control and set the culture that is followed. By having this control over the public, they can not only sway opinions and judgment but also hide certain content by deeming it wrong and therefore censoring it, leading to the controlling of mass media, while creators and users of their products are none the wiser. YouTube gains revenue from sponsors via advertisements on its videos. Due to this, YouTube is put in a unique position as it has to both satisfy other business conglomerates by supplying “suitable videos” to put ads on which, in turn, limits the videos and therefore income of the creators who were not deemed appropriate. While some videos would obviously fall under the inappropriate category, and possibly a restricted category due to its obvious breaking of YouTube’s Community Guidelines, there have been scenarios in which these terms have been used to vaguely justify their decision.

Gillespie’s article directly states how the definition of ‘platform’ has a new additional meaning, that of having to do the physical and economic workings of technology. In this, ‘platform wars’ is mentioned as it shows consumers directly putting two or more pieces of technology and devoutly picking a side e.g. Apple vs Android. Gillespie actively states how by playing into these trends, we feed the corporation’s goal of publicising and sharing their content. By acknowledging how these platforms are not just simple forms to get or post content, we are a few steps closer to unveiling the corrupt dynamics strategically and methodically done behind closed doors. Those who are impacted are mainly the consumers - people like you and me; as it is catered to us. This is due to the fact that the individuals who use these platforms to create and share content are the consumers. The ethical dynamics that come into play are due to the fact that the consumers are the ones who create but the companies are the ones who dictate what we create due to specific terms of service blocking or interfering with some content. If shareholders deem a video too graphic, sexual or crude due to the use of profanity, they will choose not to sponsor or have their ads on said video. This becomes a pattern as the creators will see they are not making money or because the video/post is not getting interacted with and therefore, will stay away from producing that content. This is a form of data manipulation because of the negative connotation associated with certain topics and the pushing of other ideologies. Obviously, if the content of the video is crude, offensive, overtly sexual etc. the content will be removed or hidden from general audiences.
Gillespie argues that due to the notion of the word ‘platform’ being used for digital enterprises, it signifies that one can use it to bolster their own messages which, in turn, creates a dynamic power play for monopolizing industries. As stated, “‘Platforms’ are ‘platforms’ not necessarily because they allow code to be written or run, but because they afford an opportunity to communicate, interact or sell” (Gillespie, 2010). Regarding identity, one can use platforms to help shape their presence online just as one would do in person. That being said, those who dominate these platforms (corporations) warp what users actively produce. YouTube acts as a vessel for advertisers to use the data from users and content creators to better tailor their products or information to audiences. The advertisers access this data directly from the corporations and are analyzed to see who or which video is gaining popularity via watch time, likes and dislike ratio, comments, and subscribers. The data is visualized by ad revenue which affects what types of content creators produce. If it is more of a niche topic, politically insensitive or deemed unacceptable for advertisers, content creators will shy away from creating videos on such topics or risk having their revenue cut. This system, while helpful for discouraging sensitive topics, also backfires as niche and smaller channels suffer from lack of engagement due to advertisers wanting to put their content on videos/media that is popular and trending at the time. The data becomes contextualized by the fact that advertisers tend to put more ads on ‘trending’ videos from bigger creators as there is a greater chance of business than on shorter videos from smaller creators. These platforms may be used by many individuals, but the corporations are the ones to dictate who or what benefits on their site. While YouTube says it is a neutral ground for everyone, sponsors and corporate partnerships sway the judgment as they provide monetary support to their site.

This article mainly presents its data in text form. All of the stats and included quotations from certain people of interest (i.e. CEO of YouTube) are written and edited by Gillespie. Users can interact with this data by either looking it up or following the citations to the listed articles used and quoted. As seen in the article, Gillespie offsets the quotations from the original text to show importance. Gillespie may have not included charts or graphs to show data, but data was shown via direct citations from other accredited journals or articles and by information from the corporations themselves (i.e. Google, Microsoft, YouTube). Overall, Gillespie actively demonstrates how the term ‘platform’ has been contextualized and used for political and power purposes via large corporations to inherently dictate users' lives.

YouTube and its reign as a crucial online platform have a very interesting development over the years. During the early years of the internet, many knew it as a "wild west" as there was not a specific staple as we see today with search engines like Google. YouTube was nothing more than just a new way to upload video content without using Myspace or Facebook and was not as popular as it is today. With this comes both positives and negatives. With it being more “mom and pop”, it leads to more genuine and a true non-biased platform. This is what a platform was before the monetization of YouTube as there were no corporations like Google or YouTube itself using/selling data of its users. This was a platform of opportunity and creativity as without the watchful eyes of mega-corporations integrating themselves into mostly every single aspect of YouTube’s system, it promoted the average person to create and upload comedy skits, skateboard tricks, singing/instrument videos etc... While you can still do these things on YouTube today, the sense of intimacy is gone and has been replaced by money seeking corporations. Many individuals who posted on YouTube had no real intention of going viral, as it was not as common as seen today, but there were some who wanted to have that one video to make them "famous".

Another form of sharing and storing older media is via archives of these different forms of virtual data to act as a digital history book. The Internet Archive is an interesting site as it allows viewers to access older versions of sites today or lost media that has been restored. Unlike a museum which has physical objects this is a digital museum of various important parts of human history online. Unfortunately, the Internet Archive has had some legal issues over the years due to copyright issues or companies trying to take certain content down. Lack of funding has become an issue as well mainly due to legal battles. This poses the main question and issue which pertains around if prior/historical data from either a currently operating site or non-operational site should be accessible due to its large historical and cultural presence. This is similar to what Gillespie spoke about as companies do not care about the "platform" as a significant aspect of history, large corporations tend to only use it as a means to gather that data for monetary gain, which ends up hurting archives like the Internet Archive as they are constantly urged to take content down. This is troubling, as we can yet again lose old data and this time for good. In an age of the technological advances that can store mass amounts of media, corporations are the ones who not only hold but dictate how they use various individuals’ data. The knowledge that corporations hold various types of personal data ranging from basic (i.e. when the account was created) to private identifiable information (i.e. name, age, location, phone number) all the while the decisions of what to do with your personal data are solely up to them. The irony in this scenario is the hypocrisy that not only can corporations like Google and YouTube dictate what to do with your information, they also attack other services like The Internet Archive for storing data they claim as their own.
