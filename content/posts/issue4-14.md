## Introduction

Although technology has its advantages and disadvantages, almost every technological advancement comes with potential risks. In the case of social media, it has a particularly significant impact on children and adolescents. This is because they might not have the experience needed to protect themselves in an environment where others can watch them whenever they want to. So one big question I have begun to ask is –

***“What are the risks of children and adolescents sharing personal information on social media platforms?”***

To answer this question, I decided to create a ”Choose Your Own Adventure” game that gives people an opportunity to experience what it might be like to face different forms of harassment as a teenager.

## Research Process

Since I was trying to understand the risks to answer the research question, I wanted to explore how harassment affects other people. One topic that kept coming up was the conflict people face when deciding whether to be a “bystander” or an “ally”, which includes the dangers and importance of each decision (Kettrey and Marx, 2018; The Oracle Editorial Board, 2021). It’s important to know the difference between the two because the outcome of a situation may depend on your actions, even when you are not the victim. This means that one of the risks of kids using social media is that they might have to make a tough decision at a young age: whether to be a bystander or an ally.

While researching, a common term that came up was FOMO (fear of missing out). This has become an increasingly recognized issue, especially for children and adolescents. Between 2018 and 2021, various studies found that social media could contribute to FOMO, which can lead to sleep difficulties and problems (Tee-Melegrito, 2023).

After getting more familiar with these topics, I wasn’t sure how to conclude the Choose Your Own Adventure games while still providing players with accurate information. I began thinking about how someone could respond to another person harassing them online. The most common recommendation I found in my research was to contact the police, which makes sense. However, another important recommendation was NOT to delete the messages or evidence sent by the harasser (Kids Help Phone, 2026; Bottaro, 2026). Therefore, another risk for children and adolescents is that they might not understand the importance of keeping evidence. Without it, they might have difficulty proving what happened.

Another risk involves the relationship between children and their parents. Although several factors can influence how children respond to online situations, parental guidance and supervision play an important role. If parents aren’t involved in teaching their child how to protect themselves online, it could lead to them exposing personal information. This might create opportunities for unwanted attention or harassment by others. But if the parents are too involved, the child may then lie about their online activities and end up having even more long-term harassment towards them. This ultimately means that when children and adolescents go on social media, there is risk that it affects the relationship between them and their parents.

After gathering enough information to develop different conclusions, I began working on how the characters would arrive at each incident. This involved creating the decision points for my Choose Your Own Adventure game, which connects to the main theme of my project: ethics. For that reason, I wanted to find an exact definition that would help me develop these decisions:

### “A system or set of moral principles; (in a weaker sense) a set of social or personal values.”

– By Brittany Loggins
(Loggins, 2026)

Given this definition, I wanted to express that everyone has different opinions and personal values when it comes to dealing with the stuff on the internet used in my game. This connects to my research question because one of the risks of social media is that people often rely on their personal beliefs when making decisions. But when people make those decisions, they are responsible for the consequences of their actions – in certain cases.

One example of how I applied this concept in my game was through Sarah Holton’s third conclusion, titled “Retaliate against him”. In the game, choosing this conclusion causes her to respond to the person harassing her through her posts. As a result, she is ‘feeding the bully’ by giving them more opportunities to harass her. She also risks losing her integrity, and if the conversation escalates, she could lose the reason that explains that she was not at fault.

In one of my earlier blog posts about ethics, I wrote about the differences between Results-Based Ethics vs. Duty-Based Ethics. In summary, Results-Based Ethics is the idea that an action is ethical only if its results are more beneficial than harmful to society. In contrast, Duty-Based Ethics is the idea that an action is right because of the feeling of responsibility to do the right thing, regardless of the consequences. For my Choose Your Own Adventure game, I wanted to create decision points that would challenge each of these arguments. With this in mind, I created a choice for players about whether they wanted to be a bystander or an ally, remain active on social media or reduce their content consumption, and react or ignore certain situations. Each of these choices might damage your character socially, but could be really damaging to someone else if not taken care of.

While I did research topics on cyberbullying, cyber sexual harassment, disinformation, racism, and hate speech, I also discovered that children face several risks outside of those issues. Personal relationships, emotional well-being, and physical safety can all be affected by experiencing online harassment. This is because sometimes the decisions being made involve the choices to be an ally, talk to parents, or take a break from social media. Although it’s not the victims fault for being harassed, the choices made before, during, and after the event can influence how the situation develops. Ultimately, it comes down to the ethical decisions young people make and which choices may be most beneficial to them, regardless of whether those decisions are considered morally right.

## Reflection:

Creating this game definitely required more effort than I thought it would, but overall, I think I did a great job. For me, the most challenging part was making the scenarios believable. In the real world however, I realized that because these situations happen suddenly online, it would be more realistic to have events also happen suddenly and without warning in the game.

Another challenge was coming up with the ideas. Developing the conclusions was pretty simple, but figuring out how to lead the players toward them was more difficult. When I was brainstorming ideas, I would literally throw an idea away, only to come back to it later because I realized it wasn’t as bad as I initially thought. This is why the papers containing the original plan for Riley Merrick are crumpled.

When building my first Choose Your Own Adventure scenario, I had a plan in mind for Sarah Holton. I realized that there were going to have to be two general topics: Conclusions 1 and 2 focus on the emotional effects of social media, including FOMO, and conclusions 4, 5, and 6 focus on the legal aspects and when to report someone. I also knew it would be best to have one option that could lead up to almost every other path, which was ‘Arguing with the Harasser’ (Conclusion 3). Besides that, developing Sarah Holton’s storyline was pretty straightforward.

My second Choose Your Own Adventure scenario focused on Riley Merrick. I followed the same structure as the previous scenario, with my two main topics being ally/bystander and catfishing/grooming, while my secondary option focused on talking to the parents. This scenario took a little longer than the previous one because I had to provide more details about my two main topics to give players enough content. For example, in the grooming/catfishing scenario, I explained that the person did several things that suggested ‘Shadow Hunter’ wasn’t who they claimed to be. This included stalking, making inconsistent statements, and always agreeing with the player’s character. Additionally, my placeholder name was Re-Re, then Riley Jution, but it eventually changed into Riley Merrick, which is why you see those names written on the papers.
